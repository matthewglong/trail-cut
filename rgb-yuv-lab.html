§ 01

A color, written in light

Every color on a screen is a recipe for three lamps. Add red, green, and blue light in the right proportions and the eye reads a single hue. This is the additive model — start from black and add light until you arrive.

The triplet on the right is the raw material for everything that follows. Move it. The big swatch is the literal color; the numbers beneath are the same color spoken in different dialects.

Bit depth decides how finely each lamp is quantized — 8-bit gives 256 steps (0–255), 10-bit gives 1024, 12-bit 4096. Toggle it in the signal panel above and the code values below stretch; the color is identical, only the ruler changes.

Stored ≠ light. The value you store is not proportional to photons — it is bent by a transfer function (the "gamma" curve) so that codes are spent where the eye is sensitive. The relative luminance below decodes that curve back to linear light; §5 returns to this.
§ 02

Pulling brightness apart from color

Human vision is mostly a brightness instrument. We carry far more light-sensitive rod cells than color-sensitive cones, so we notice a smudge in brightness long before a smudge in color.

Y′ — luma — is the brightness axis: a weighted sum of R, G, and B. It is roughly what a black-and-white television would show. The two chroma axes, U and V, carry only the color that is left over once brightness is removed — scaled versions of (B − Y′) and (R − Y′).

This split is the whole trick behind video compression. Keep Y′ at full resolution, then throw away three quarters of the U/V samples (the 4:2:0 scheme) — and the eye scarcely complains.

§ 03

The transform, and where it comes from

The conversion is one matrix multiplication. The only freedom in it is three numbers — Kr, Kg, Kb — the luma weights. And those are not invented; they are derived from the primaries.

Step 1 — pick primaries and a white point. A color space is defined by the chromaticities (the x, y coordinates on the CIE 1931 diagram) of its three primary lights, plus the white that results when all three fire equally. The table below reflects the primaries axis you chose above.

Step 2 — solve for luminance. Given those primaries there is exactly one matrix mapping linear RGB to CIE XYZ. Its middle row — the Y row — is the matched Kr, Kg, Kb. No tuning.

Why is green ≈ 70%? Because the eye peaks in sensitivity near 555 nm, deep in the green. The luminous-efficiency curve V(λ) below shows it — green carries the brightness.

Step 3 — scale the color differences. With the weights fixed, U and V are simply defined as:

U = (B − Y′) ÷ [ 2·(1 − Kb) ]
V = (R − Y′) ÷ [ 2·(1 − Kr) ]

The 2·(1−K) divisors squeeze both axes into [−0.5, +0.5] for every legal RGB input, so U and V share Y′'s dynamic range — convenient for packing into equal bit budgets.

§ 05

Luma is not luminance — and what HDR changes

The Y′ on the scope is a convenient fiction. It is a weighted sum of the gamma-encoded R′G′B′ values — brightness computed in the wrong domain, because it is cheap and the eye forgives it.

True luminance Y is different: decode each channel through the transfer curve back to linear light, then weight by the primaries. That is the photometric brightness. Watch the two diverge as you move the specimen — the gap is the price of doing the sum before undoing gamma.

The transfer function is the sixth axis. It maps a stored code to the light it represents. sRGB and BT.1886 are SDR curves (≈ 2.2–2.4 gamma). HDR needs more range, so it uses different curves: PQ (absolute, calibrated up to 10 000 cd/m²) or HLG (scene-referred, broadcast-friendly).

So why does HDR keep BT.2020 primaries? Because gamut and brightness-curve are independent axes. BT.2100 — the HDR standard — is literally "BT.2020 primaries + a PQ or HLG curve." Same colors available; a new ruler for brightness. That orthogonality is the whole point of this section.

Note: the swatch can only show the stored code as sRGB — an ordinary monitor cannot display PQ/HLG light — so picking an HDR curve changes the luminance math, not the on-screen color.

Why split into luma and chroma at all?

Because vision is asymmetric. We resolve fine detail in brightness far better than in color. Codecs keep full-resolution Y′ and quietly downsample U and V. The common 4:2:0 layout discards three-quarters of the chroma samples — invisible on most footage, and a large share of the file-size win.

The coefficients are physics, not taste

For the current matrix, the luma weights are the measured relative luminance of each primary under its gamut and white point:

Kr = 0.2126 · Kg = 0.7152 · Kb = 0.0722
